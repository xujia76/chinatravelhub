Culture & Customs

What the locals know

Festivals, tea, temples, and the small unwritten rules of daily life — understand these and China stops being exotic and starts being home.

The Unwritten Rules

Live like a local

Every culture has rules nobody writes down. These eight are the ones that matter most for visitors — get them right and every interaction warms up.

Nationwide · Jan/Feb

Chinese New Year

The biggest annual homecoming on earth — reunion dinners, red packets, and a week of firecrackers.

Chinese New Year (Spring Festival) is a 15-day festival that empties the cities: millions travel home, families cook the year's best meal, elders give red packets (hongbao), and the country pauses to reset.

The reunion dinner on New Year's Eve is the sacred centerpiece — dumplings in the north, fish (never finished, for surplus) in the south, always more dishes than anyone can eat.

Red packets of money are given to children and unmarried adults; the amount should be even and auspicious — avoid 4, which sounds like 'death'.

Expect firecrackers at midnight and morning greetings of 'gong xi fa cai' (wish you wealth). Shops close for the first week — plan food and cash ahead if you travel then.

If invited to a family dinner, arrive early, compliment the food, and take a small gift — fruit or tea, never clocks or umbrellas.

Traveler tip: Travelling during this period is magical but crowded — trains book out weeks ahead, so fix your dates early. Many museums are closed days 1–3.
Nationwide · Sep/Oct

Mid-Autumn Festival

The full-moon festival — lanterns, mooncakes, and the night the whole country looks up.

Mid-Autumn falls on the full moon of the eighth lunar month: families gather to eat mooncakes, drink tea, and watch the moon — the roundest, brightest symbol of reunion in Chinese culture.

Mooncakes are exchanged obsessively in the weeks before — companies gift boxes, families swap flavors. The classic is lotus paste with a salted egg yolk.

Cities hold lantern displays; the best are in old towns and park lakes, where lanterns float on the water. Children carry small paper lanterns on the night itself.

If you are invited to share the festival, bring tea or fruit — and expect to be offered mooncake you cannot refuse (eat a slice, even if sweet).

Traveler tip: The moon is best watched at 9–10pm from a lakeside or rooftop — and yes, the 'rabbit in the moon' legend will be enthusiastically explained to you.
Nationwide · May/Jun

Dragon Boat Festival

Racing boats, sticky-rice pyramids, and a 2,000-year-old story of loyalty.

Dragon Boat Festival honors Qu Yuan, a poet of 2,300 years ago who drowned himself in protest — the legend says villagers raced out in boats and threw rice to feed the fish. Today: zongzi and dragon-boat races.

Zongzi are pyramid-shaped parcels of glutinous rice — wrapped in bamboo or reed leaves, stuffed with pork and yolk (south) or red dates (north), boiled for hours.

Dragon-boat races happen on rivers across southern China; the most spectacular are in Guangdong and Hunan. Arrive early for the best bank spot.

Homes hang calamus and mugwort at the door for protection — you will see the bundles even in modern cities.

Traveler tip: Zongzi are eaten warm with sugar or soy — and the leaf is a wrapper, not food. One parcel is a meal; two is a feast.
Tea country nationwide

The Tea Culture

Not a drink — a language. Learn the ritual and the whole country opens up.

Tea is China's most universal social currency. From a street vendor's glass of jasmine to a formal gongfu ceremony, tea is how people slow down, do business, and show respect.

The map: green teas from Zhejiang and Jiangxi (Longjing is the crown jewel), oolong from Fujian (Tieguanyin), black tea from Fujian and Yunnan, and pu-erh, the fermented aged tea from Yunnan that collectors treat like wine.

The gongfu ritual: rinse the cups, warm the pot, pour the tea in small bitter-sweet sips. The host pours; the guest taps two fingers on the table to say thanks.

In a teahouse, your pot is refilled with hot water until the leaves give out — a session can last hours for the price of one pot.

A gift of good tea is the most respected gesture in Chinese social life. If you visit a tea village, the tasting is free and the pressure is gentle.

Traveler tip: Accept the first pour even if you dislike tea — refusing is read as refusing the relationship. One sip, then honesty is welcome.
Buddhist & Taoist sites

Temple Etiquette

How to visit temples with respect — and what the incense and bows actually mean.

China's temples — Buddhist, Taoist, and local shrines — are living places of worship, not just photo stops. A few simple habits make your visit welcomed rather than endured.

Dress modestly: covered shoulders and knees. Remove hats inside halls. If incense is offered, light it from the flame side, hold it at forehead height, and bow three times before placing it in the burner.

Do not point at statues with one finger — gesture with an open palm. Do not take photos of statues in some halls; look for the no-camera sign or ask.

Walk clockwise around pagodas and stupas. In the main hall, step over (never on) the high threshold — it separates the sacred from the everyday.

A donation is optional; a few yuan in the box with a silent wish is the traditional way.

Traveler tip: Early morning (7–9am) is when temples feel alive — locals doing morning prayers, monks sweeping, incense fresh. Midday is for tourists.
Every table, everywhere

Dining Etiquette

The rules that make or break a Chinese dinner — chopsticks, toasts, and the shared table.

Chinese meals are shared affairs with rules that feel natural within minutes. Learn the core ones and you will eat like an insider everywhere.

Never stick chopsticks upright in a bowl of rice — it resembles funeral incense. Rest them on the chopstick rest or the side of the bowl.

When food is served, let the host or elders start first. Use the serving chopsticks for shared dishes, not your own.

Toasting: hold your cup slightly lower than the host's when clinking. 'Ganbei' means bottoms up; for everything else, sip. Never pour your own drink — pour for others and let them pour for you.

The bill: everyone reaches for it, the host insists. If a colleague insists on paying, accept with thanks — offering again later is the polite return.

Traveler tip: Saying 'xie xie' and leaving a little food on your plate signals you are full and satisfied — a clean plate can read as 'still hungry'.
Nationwide

Red Packets & Gifts

The color red speaks louder than words — a quick guide to giving and receiving well.

Hongbao (red packets) and gift-giving carry precise social meaning in China. Getting the basics right earns instant goodwill; getting them wrong can quietly offend.

Red packets: given at New Year, weddings, and births — cash in a red envelope, never white (funerals) or black. Amounts should be even, avoid 4, and favor 8 or 6.

Gifts: bring something from your home country — tea, coffee, or local sweets are safe. Avoid clocks ('song zhong' sounds like attending a funeral) and pears ('li' sounds like separation).

Present and receive gifts with two hands. Do not open a gift immediately unless encouraged — opening later is polite in many circles.

When you receive a red packet or gift, modest refusal first ('bu yong, bu yong') is traditional — the giver insists, and you accept with thanks.

Traveler tip: If you give money, use crisp new bills — old or folded notes are considered careless. And a small red packet to a guide or host's child is always warmly received.
Every Chinese city

The Morning Park

Tai chi, badminton, opera, and chess — the daily life that no guidebook schedules.

Between 6 and 9am, Chinese parks become the country's real living room: retired couples dancing, tai chi masters moving through forms, badminton players improvising nets, opera singers practicing against the willows.

The morning park is free, open, and welcoming — join a tai chi group with a smile and you will likely be taught a few moves. Language is not required; mimicry is accepted.

Dancers dominate after 7am — 'square dancing' with music boxes. No one minds if you watch or even join the back row.

Tea houses inside parks fill by 8am with chess and card players — a 'kan' (looker) crowd gathers around every good game.

This is the least touristy China you will see in a week. Go with no plan, sit on a bench, and watch an hour pass.

Traveler tip: Bring nothing but a water bottle. The best morning-park cities for visitors: Chengdu (People's Park), Hangzhou (by West Lake), Beijing (Temple of Heaven grounds).
