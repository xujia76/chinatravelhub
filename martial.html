Chinese Martial Arts

The way of the fist — and the breath

Tai chi, kung fu, qigong, and the great internal arts — where they come from, what they mean, and exactly where you can see or learn them.

Nationwide · parks & temples

Tai Chi

The martial art that looks like meditation — slow, flowing, and secretly lethal.

Tai chi (taijiquan) is China's most practiced martial art: slow, continuous movements that train alignment, balance, and internal power. Millions practice it every morning in parks — and it is a real fighting system underneath the calm.

The five major families — Chen, Yang, Wu, Wu (Hao), and Sun — share the same principles but different flavors: Chen has explosive fajin bursts, Yang is the slow grand form most people picture.

The martial core is the 'silk reeling' spiral and the push-hands (tuishou) partner drill — learning to yield, redirect, and issue force through structure, not muscle.

For visitors: join any morning park group and you will be welcomed; just mirror the leader. Parks like Beijing's Temple of Heaven and Chengdu's People's Park have strong daily groups.

A week of classes will change how you stand and breathe; a year changes how you move through the world.

Traveler tip: Wear flat, flexible shoes and arrive before 7am — the groups dissolve by 8, replaced by dancers and chess players.
Dengfeng, Henan

Shaolin Kung Fu

The temple that birthed a thousand styles — monks, legends, and the cradle of northern kung fu.

Shaolin Temple in Henan is the most famous martial-arts site on earth: a Buddhist monastery that has trained warrior monks for 1,500 years, and the ancestral home of northern Shaolin kung fu.

The Shaolin curriculum spans bare-hand forms, staff, and the 'eighteen weapons', plus the animal styles — tiger, crane, snake, leopard, and dragon — each teaching a different fighting logic.

The temple's martial lineage famously inspired Bruce Lee, Jet Li, and the modern wuxia imagination. The surrounding Dengfeng area hosts several martial schools where foreigners train year-round.

Visit early, watch a monk demonstration (several are held daily), and respect the rule: it is a working monastery, so dress modestly and skip flash photography in the halls.

If you want to actually train, dozens of schools in the region offer week-long 'kung fu experience' packages for all levels.

Traveler tip: The monk demonstrations feel like theatre — they are also the real thing. Arrive 30 minutes early for a front spot; the courtyard fills fast.
Guangdong / Hong Kong

Wing Chun

Close-range, efficient, street-born — the style Ip Man made famous.

Wing Chun is a southern style built for close combat: no wasted motion, an economy of movement, and a centerline theory that says attack and defense happen along the shortest line — straight at your opponent's chest.

Made legendary by Ip Man and his student Bruce Lee, Wing Chun is compact enough to fight in a phone booth: simultaneous defense and strike, sticky hands (chi sao) to feel an opponent's intent through touch.

The signature stance is the YGKYM ('goat-clamping') horse — low, narrow, and rooted. Beginners drill the three basic hand forms and the wooden dummy.

In Guangdong and Hong Kong, many private masters still teach the traditional curriculum; classes are small, serious, and often conducted in a mix of Cantonese and English.

It is the least 'showy' martial art — which is exactly the point: efficiency over spectacle.

Traveler tip: A chi sao session with a good master is humbling and addictive — expect to be 'stuck' and gently thrown for the first hour. That is the lesson.
Nationwide

Qigong

Breathing as medicine — the ancient practice behind every Chinese martial art.

Qigong ('qi work') is the cultivation practice at the root of all Chinese martial arts: coordinated breath, posture, and intention to build internal energy, health, and calm. No sweating required — and that is the point.

The two great branches: stillness qigong (standing like a tree — zhan zhuang) and moving qigong (flowing forms like the famous Eight Pieces of Brocade, baduanjin).

Modern Chinese hospitals and clinics use qigong-style therapy alongside medicine; millions practice daily, and it is a mainstay of park mornings and rehabilitation programs.

For visitors, the most accessible entry is baduanjin — eight gentle movements, ten minutes, doable in a hotel room. Classes are common in parks, teahouses, and wellness centers.

The science-friendly framing: qigong trains breath control, posture, and focus — the 'qi' is felt as warmth and flow. Whether you call it energy or biomechanics, the practice works.

Traveler tip: Learn the Eight Pieces of Brocade before you arrive — five minutes of YouTube daily for a week, and you can join any morning park group as an insider.
Shanxi / Hebei / nationwide

Xingyi Quan

The straight-line art of soldiers — five elements, one punch philosophy, terrifying economy.

Xingyi ('form-intent') is the most direct of the internal arts: no circles, no retreat — a straight-line style built on the 'five elements' (piercing, splitting, drilling, chopping, crossing) and twelve animal forms.

Its origin is soldierly: legend traces it to Marshal Yue Fei, and the style's stance-to-stance logic mirrors spear work. Practitioners say xingyi is the shortest distance between two points — applied to combat.

The 'three-body posture' (santishi) is its foundation: hours of standing to build the frame that makes every punch arrive with the whole body behind it.

Xingyi pairs naturally with bagua zhang in traditional practice — many masters of the two arts were the same people, taught together as complementary opposites.

It is less common than tai chi in parks, but a serious xingyi practitioner is unmistakable: explosive, linear, and eerily still between movements.

Traveler tip: Ask a practitioner to show you the 'split' element — one movement. It looks simple and lands like a freight train. Do not volunteer to receive it.
Beijing / nationwide

Bagua Zhang

Walking circles, changing palms — the martial art that moves like weather.

Bagua zhang ('eight-trigram palm') is the circular internal art: practitioners walk a circle, endlessly changing direction and palm techniques, turning the opponent's aggression into their own motion.

Its philosophy mirrors the I Ching's eight trigrams — constant change. You never meet force with force; you walk around it, behind it, through it.

The signature skill is 'dragon body' — a low, coiled walk with the head high, like a snake wrapped around a tree. Footwork is the art; the palms are a bonus.

Historically associated with Beijing's bodyguard culture (the palace escort lore), it remains one of the most visually striking arts to watch in parks.

It complements xingyi in the classical pairing: one straight, one circular — together they are the complete internal curriculum.

Traveler tip: Park masters love showing off bagua's circling walk to curious foreigners — watch the feet, not the hands. The hands are the decoy.
Hong Kong heritage · worldwide

Jeet Kune Do & Bruce Lee

The philosophy that broke every rule — Bruce Lee's anti-style style.

Jeet Kune Do is not a style but an idea, forged by Bruce Lee: absorb what is useful, reject what is useless, add what is uniquely your own. 'Be like water' — adapt, flow, and strike when the opening is there.

Lee built JKD from Wing Chun, fencing, boxing, and Filipino martial arts, stripping away ceremony in favor of what works — intercepting the opponent's attack rather than blocking it.

In Hong Kong, his legacy lives at the Bruce Lee statue on the Avenue of Stars and in the old Kowloon sites of his story. His films — Enter the Dragon, Fist of Fury — remain the gateway drug for global kung fu obsession.

JKD is taught worldwide; in China, the legacy is more philosophical than institutional — the idea, not the curriculum.

For visitors, the takeaway is practical: watch his interviews, not just the fights. His philosophy of flow applies to travel, business, and everything else.

Traveler tip: The Bruce Lee statue at the Avenue of Stars is free, iconic, and best visited at sunset — and yes, everyone does the water-cup salute photo.
Wudang Mountains, Hubei

Wudang & the Taoist Arts

Where kung fu meets Taoism — mist, mountains, and the art of yielding.

The Wudang Mountains are the sacred home of Taoist martial arts: the birthplace of tai chi's internal lineage in legend, and a living center where Taoist monks still practice sword, tai chi, and qigong on forested peaks.

The visual is unmatched: Taoist temples on cliff tops above cloud seas, practitioners practicing sword forms at dawn. The Golden Summit at 1,612m is the pilgrimage's crown.

Wudang's arts are internal: taiji, xingyi, bagua, and the legendary Wudang sword — taught with a Taoist worldview of balance, breath, and non-contention.

Several academies in the mountains accept foreigners for multi-week training immersions — accommodation, meals, and daily classes in the clouds.

It is a place to feel the philosophy: every form is a meditation, every step a negotiation with gravity.

Traveler tip: Go in spring or autumn for clear skies; the cloud-sea days are gorgeous but the summit can be socked in. Trainers at the academies teach in English and love genuine beginners.
