Chinese Food

Twelve dishes worth the trip

Food is the fastest way into any culture. These are the regional icons — where they come from, how to eat them, and what not to do. Click any dish for the full story.

The Regional Icons

Eat like a local

Chinese food is regional, not national. Each dish below is a doorway into its city — order the classic, watch the ritual, and the locals will warm to you instantly.

Beijing

Peking Duck

A theatrical dish — crispy lacquered skin carved tableside and wrapped in thin pancakes.

Peking duck is the closest Chinese food gets to theatre. The duck is roasted until its skin is glass-crisp, then carved at your table, skin and meat separated, served with paper-thin pancakes, sweet bean sauce, and slivers of scallion and cucumber.

Order the classic three-part eating ritual: skin dipped in sugar (the purest crunch), then a pancake wrap with sauce and scallions, and finally the remaining meat in a stir-fry — most restaurants suggest the duck bone soup as the closing course.

The best-known houses (like Quanjude and Siji Minfu in Beijing) roast over fruitwood for that signature aroma. A whole duck feeds two to three people generously.

Book a table early — lunch queues start before noon at the famous houses. Ask for the 'skin first' presentation if you want the full ritual.

Traveler tip: Ask your waiter to show you the duck before it is carved — it is tradition, and the photo is half the meal.
Xi'an

Xi'an Lamb Paomo

A lamb stew you build yourself — tear the flatbread, hand it back, and wait for the broth.

Paomo is Xi'an's slow ritual: you tear a round flatbread into tiny crumbs with your fingers (the smaller, the better), hand the bowl back, and the kitchen fills it with simmering lamb broth, vermicelli, and tender mutton.

Tearing the bread is part of the experience — locals take it seriously. The finer you tear, the more flavor the bread absorbs; a relaxed tear-and-chat session is the correct pace.

The best bowls come from the old Muslim Quarter stalls, where the broth has been simmering all morning. Order 'yao tang' (more broth) or 'gan pao' (drier) to tune the consistency.

Eat it with a side of pickled garlic and chilli oil — the tang cuts the richness of the lamb.

Traveler tip: Do not rush the bread-tearing. A hurried bowl is a lesser bowl — this is a dish built on patience.
Chengdu / Chongqing

Sichuan Hotpot

A bubbling cauldron at the center of the table — the communal heart of Sichuan dining.

Hotpot is less a dish than a social event: a pot of bubbling broth — half spicy, half clear for the cautious — surrounded by raw ingredients you cook yourself at the table.

The signature is the ma-la (numbing-spicy) broth built on Sichuan peppercorn and dried chillies. First-timers should start with a split pot: spicy on one side, tomato or mushroom on the other.

The classic dipping sauce is a small bowl of sesame oil with minced garlic — it cools the spice and lifts every ingredient. Soy, vinegar, and cilantro are optional additions.

Typical ingredients: sliced beef, tripe, lotus root, potato, enoki mushrooms, and the Sichuan specialty of fresh duck blood. Cook beef in seconds, vegetables in minutes.

Traveler tip: Never drink cold water to fight the spice — sip the house tea or beer instead. And let the oil sauce do its job; it is the secret weapon.
Guangzhou / Hong Kong

Cantonese Dim Sum

A thousand small dishes, pushed past your table on trolleys — breakfast as a long, lazy affair.

Dim sum is Cantonese morning culture: small plates of dumplings, buns, and pastries, ordered pot of tea at a time, from morning until early afternoon.

Start with the classics: har gow (crystal shrimp dumplings), siu mai (pork and shrimp), char siu bao (barbecue pork buns), and cheung fun (silky rice rolls).

Traditional halls push trolleys of bamboo steamers past your table — point at what catches your eye. Modern houses use ticketed menus; both are authentic, just different generations.

Pair everything with a pot of pu-erh or oolong. The tea is not a drink; it is the rhythm of the meal.

Traveler tip: Go in a group of four to six if you can — the joy of dim sum is ordering ten dishes and sharing everything.
Shanghai

Shanghai Xiaolongbao

A soup-filled parcel no bigger than a coin purse — bite carefully, it bites back.

Xiaolongbao is Shanghai in one mouthful: a thin pleated wrapper holding seasoned pork and a spoonful of hot broth, sealed inside by frozen gelatin that melts on steaming.

The correct first bite is a small puncture in the top to release the steam, then a sip of the broth before the rest. Biting straight in is a guaranteed tongue burn.

Classic partners: black vinegar with slivered ginger on the side — dip the whole dumpling, not just the top.

The best shops steam to order; a queue of locals outside is the quality signal. Din Tai Fung made the style world-famous, but Shanghai's old teahouse-style shops do it with more soul.

Traveler tip: Count the pleats — traditional xiaolongbao have 18. It is not essential, just a piece of the romance.
Lanzhou / nationwide

Lanzhou Beef Noodles

One clear broth, one clean bowl — the noodle breakfast that conquered a nation.

Lanzhou lamian is China's most democratic noodle: a clear beef broth, hand-pulled noodles, a few slices of beef, radish, coriander, and chilli oil — nothing more, and nothing less.

The noodles are pulled fresh to order: thin, medium, or wide — 'xi, er, da' — plus specialty shapes. Watch the puller through the open kitchen; it is a performance in itself.

The broth's clarity is the point. A good bowl tastes of beef and bones, not seasoning. Add chilli oil to your taste — 'bu yao la' if you want it mild.

It is a breakfast and lunch staple across China; the best shops in Lanzhou itself have queues that start at 6am.

Traveler tip: Choose 'jiu ye' (wide, flat ribbons) for texture or 'xi xi de' (hair-thin) for broth absorption — and eat fast; noodles wait for no one.
Chengdu

Mapo Tofu

Silky tofu in a red oil that numbs and burns at once — Sichuan's most famous export.

Mapo tofu is the perfect Sichuan dish: tender tofu cubes braised in a fiery sauce of doubanjiang (fermented broad-bean paste), minced beef, and a generous dose of ground Sichuan peppercorn.

The name honors a pockmarked grandmother ('mapo') who invented it in Chengdu in the 19th century. The authentic version is far more complex than the takeout standard — the doubanjiang is stir-fried until the oil runs red.

Eat it over plain rice; the sauce is meant to be soaked up. The numbing (ma) comes last, after the spice — that is the Sichuan signature.

Order 'la du shao yi dian' if you want less heat, but understand you are missing the point — the balance of numbing and spicy is the dish.

Traveler tip: The tell of a great mapo tofu: the oil is red and glossy, and the pepper's numbness lingers a few seconds after each spoonful.
Xi'an

Roujiamo

China's original hamburger — slow-braised pork stuffed into a crisp flatbread.

Roujiamo is Xi'an's answer to the sandwich: braised pork shoulder and offal, chopped and stuffed into a warm, crispy flatbread (baiji mo) baked in a clay oven.

The best versions braise the meat for hours in a spiced broth. Ask for 'fei shou' (fatty-lean mix) — the fat is what makes it unforgettable.

It pairs with a bowl of liangpi (cold noodles) or a glass of sour plum juice in the Muslim Quarter.

The bread should be crisp outside and tender inside; a sad, chewy bun is the sign of a shortcut shop.

Traveler tip: Eat it within five minutes of it being made — the contrast of hot crisp bread and warm braised meat is the whole point.
Guilin

Guilin Rice Noodles

The breakfast that runs Guilin — tender rice noodles in a secret-recipe sauce.

Guilin mifen is the city's morning fuel: soft rice noodles tossed in a savory sauce with pickled bamboo shoots, peanuts, and your choice of pork or beef, served dry or in broth.

The soul is the lu shui — a fermented sauce that every shop guards like a family secret. Two shops two streets apart taste completely different.

Order it 'ban' (mixed dry) with a bowl of clear broth on the side, and add pickled long beans, chilli, and fresh coriander to your liking.

A local breakfast costs the price of a coffee — one of China's best value meals, and it fuels a day of karst hiking.

Traveler tip: Stand at the counter with the locals instead of taking a table — the fastest way to learn which toppings the regulars actually use.
Kunming

Yunnan Cross-Bridge Noodles

A bowl of boiling broth and a ritual of raw ingredients that cook at your table.

Guoqiao mifen (crossing-the-bridge noodles) is Yunnan's most theatrical breakfast: a vast bowl of bubbling broth, plus small plates of raw sliced chicken, fish, vegetables, and rice noodles that you slide in yourself, watching them cook.

The name comes from a scholar who crossed a bridge daily to study on an island; his wife kept his food warm by floating a layer of oil on the broth — the 'bridge' is between the bank and the island.

The order matters: meat and eggs first (they need the hottest oil), then vegetables, then noodles, then a patient wait.

The broth stays hot because of the oil seal — do not drink it straight from the bowl at first; blow and test with a spoon.

Traveler tip: Add ingredients one course at a time, not all at once — the ritual is half the flavor, and the oil layer keeps the broth lethal for minutes.
Tianjin / nationwide

Jianbing

The 5-minute street breakfast — a crisp crepe folded around egg, scallion, and a crunchy cracker.

Jianbing is China's ultimate street breakfast: a thin mung-bean crepe cooked on a round griddle, an egg cracked over it, brushed with hoisin-style sauce and chilli, scattered with scallion and coriander, and folded around a crispy fried cracker (bao cui).

The order goes: 'jianbing, one egg, no coriander, extra chilli' — every vendor has their own sauce recipe, and locals have opinions.

It is a north-China breakfast that has spread nationwide; the best are made at busy intersections where the griddle never cools.

Eat it immediately, walking — the contrast of hot crepe, cold morning, and crunchy cracker is the experience.

Traveler tip: Ask for 'jia bao cui' (double cracker) if you want it extra crunchy — and hold the plastic bag open so it steams less; crispness is everything.
Mid-Autumn, nationwide

Mooncake

The pastry of the Mid-Autumn Festival — dense, sweet, and loaded with meaning.

Mooncakes appear once a year, for the Mid-Autumn Festival: small dense pastries with a rich filling — lotus seed paste, red bean, or the classic salted egg yolk — traditionally given as gifts to family, friends, and colleagues.

The traditional Cantonese mooncake has a golden crust and a single salted egg yolk at its heart, symbolizing the full moon. Northerners eat a flakier version; modern shops make everything from ice-cream to matcha.

Eat them in thin slices — they are intensely sweet and rich. Pair with a pot of jasmine or pu-erh tea, which is the proper companion.

If you travel during the festival, accepting a mooncake (even a small one) is a warm social ritual — and gift etiquette runs: never refuse twice.

Traveler tip: One mooncake serves four people at tea. And the salted-egg version is the benchmark — judge a box by its yolk.
