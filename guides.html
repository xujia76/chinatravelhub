Travel Guides

The practical basics

Eight guides that remove every friction point of traveling China — read these once and the rest is easy.

Know before you go

Read these first

Visas, payments, trains, translation, hotels, food, safety, and connectivity — the complete pre-departure pack, 2026 edition.

Visas & entry

China's entry rules have been changing fast. Here is how to get it right in 2026.

For many nationalities, visa-free entry is now the default.

Most European, Australian, New Zealand, Japanese, and South Korean passports currently enjoy 15–30 day visa-free stays (as of early 2026). Always verify the current list with your nearest Chinese embassy or the official China Visa service site before booking.

If your passport needs a visa, apply at least 4–6 weeks ahead. The standard L (tourist) visa now allows multiple entries, and applications can be done online at many consulates. Keep a printed copy of your flight and hotel bookings for immigration.

Carry your passport with you, and register your accommodation — your hotel does this automatically. If you stay in a private apartment, the host must register you with the local police station within 24 hours.

The 24-hour and 144-hour transit visa-free rules still apply at major hubs: you can often stay 2–6 days in cities like Shanghai, Beijing, and Guangzhou without a visa, if you hold a confirmed onward ticket.

Insider tip: Take a photo of your passport page and visa on your phone. It is accepted in most hotel check-ins and saves a fumble at immigration.

Mobile payments

China is nearly cashless. Set up Alipay or WeChat Pay in 15 minutes — before you land.

Alipay and WeChat Pay accept international Visa and Mastercard on foreign cards.

Download both apps, choose 'International' mode, and link your card before departure — the process is smoother on home Wi-Fi.

Both apps now support English interfaces, and Alipay has a dedicated Traveler Pass. For taxis, hotels, street food, and train tickets, a QR scan settles everything in seconds.

Cash is still legal and accepted, but carry small denominations — many street vendors cannot make change for large notes, and some smaller shops are cash-free entirely.

Keep one card that does not charge foreign fees, and tell your bank you are traveling. Withdrawing 200–300 yuan in cash at an airport ATM is a sensible backup.

Insider tip: Add a second card as backup in Alipay — if your primary card is blocked by your bank's fraud filter, the fix is a 2-minute switch, not a call home.

Getting around

High-speed rail is the best travel bargain in the world. Ride it like a local.

China's high-speed network (G and D trains) connects every major city.

Beijing–Shanghai in 4.5 hours, Beijing–Xi'an in 4.5, Chengdu–Chongqing in 1. China Railway (12306.cn) sells tickets online with a passport; the official app has an English mode.

Book long-distance trains 3–7 days ahead during holidays. At the station, scan your passport at the gate — no paper ticket needed for domestic routes on the app-booking system.

Metro systems in 40+ cities are cheap (1.5–4 yuan), frequent, and English-labeled. Use a China-friendly map app rather than a western one for transit directions.

For cities without rail links (many western destinations), domestic flights are affordable. Fly into Lijiang, Zhangjiajie, and Guilin directly — rail detours add a day.

Insider tip: A 90-minute 'D' train costs about the same as an economy flight for 4-hour routes — and arrives downtown, not at a distant airport. Take the train whenever the rail time is under 5 hours.

Language & translation

You can travel China with zero Chinese. These tools and phrases carry you through.

Translation apps are excellent now — DeepL and the major AI translators handle Chinese well.

Photo-translation (point at a menu or sign) is remarkably accurate. Download an offline pack before you land.

A dozen phrases make a real difference: ni hao (hello), xie xie (thank you), bu yao la (not spicy), dui bu qi (sorry), and 'this one, please' — pointing works.

For addresses and taxi destinations, have the Chinese characters ready on your phone screen — drivers and maps both read Chinese far better than pinyin.

Most young urbanites speak some English, and hotel staff, metro staff, and airport counters are reliably bilingual. In smaller towns, the translation app is your companion.

Insider tip: Screenshot the Chinese name of your hotel and key sights before each day. Showing a screen beats pronouncing pinyin nine times out of ten.

Where to stay

From international chains to courtyard guesthouses — how to choose, and how to book.

International sites (Booking, Agoda, Trip.com) show English reviews and handle most hotels.

Domestic guesthouses and 'minsu' often appear only on Chinese platforms — use Trip.com's local inventory for the widest range.

For old-town destinations like Lijiang and Yangshuo, a courtyard guesthouse (minsu) beats a chain hotel: quieter rooms, local hosts, and breakfast with a view. Check recent reviews for noise and dampness — old buildings vary.

Location beats star rating. In Beijing, stay near a metro line inside the 2nd ring; in Xi'an, inside the city wall; in Shanghai, near a metro station anywhere in the former French Concession or Bund area.

Hotels register your passport at check-in — this is automatic and required by law. Keep your booking confirmation in Chinese ready for taxi drivers.

Insider tip: Book the first night of a trip in advance; for the rest, walk-in is often fine off-season and gets you better prices than same-day apps.

Food & dining

Chinese food is regional, not national. Order like a local and eat better than any list.

The regional map: Beijing (roast duck, lamb hotpot), Xi'an (noodles, roujiamo), Sichuan (hotpot, mapo tofu), Hunan (steaming spice), Cantonese (dim sum, slow soups), Shanghai (xiaolongbao).

A 'national dish' does not exist — regional is the only way. Ordering basics: point at the menu with photos, say 'zhe ge' (this one), and specify 'bu yao la' (not spicy) if needed.

For hotpot, a split pot (half spicy, half clear) keeps everyone happy. Street food is safe when it is cooked in front of you and busy.

Follow the queues of locals — a long line at 7pm is the best quality signal in China. Tea houses and night markets are dining experiences, not just stops. In Chengdu, the teahouse IS the main course.

Insider tip: Chopstick rule: never stick chopsticks upright in a bowl of rice — it resembles funeral incense. Rest them on the chopstick rest instead.

Safety & etiquette

China is one of the safest large countries to travel. A little etiquette keeps every interaction warm.

Street crime is rare. Petty theft exists in tourist crowds — keep your phone in a front pocket at busy sites.

Personal space and physical contact differ: strangers will not bump you on purpose, and loud disagreements in public are rare. Keep your own voice moderate in queues and on transport.

In temples and mosques, dress conservatively and remove hats where signs ask. Do not photograph people without a smile and a gesture first — most are happy to say yes.

Tipping is not expected anywhere, including restaurants and hotels. Offering a small gift (fruit, tea) to a homestay host is the generous equivalent.

Insider tip: In an emergency, dial 110 (police), 120 (ambulance), or 119 (fire) — operators can usually connect to an English speaker. Your hotel front desk is the fastest interpreter.

Internet & maps

The apps that make China easy — and what to know about connectivity before you go.

Install a China-friendly map app before arrival — western map apps are unreliable or incomplete in China.

The local map app (with English mode) is essential for metro routing and walking directions.

Translation: DeepL and the major AI translation apps work well offline once language packs are downloaded. Photo-translate menus and signs by pointing your camera.

Connectivity: international roaming from your home carrier works in China and keeps your usual apps reachable — but can be pricey. Travel eSIMs sold before departure are the popular middle ground; hotel and café Wi-Fi is widespread but may be slow.

Local SIM cards (China Mobile, China Unicom) at airport counters are cheap and give fast data — bring your passport, and ask for a tourist data plan with a QR-setup.

Insider tip: Whatever connectivity you choose, download offline maps of your first two cities and screenshots of your hotel address before you fly. It makes the first hour stress-free.

How not to be ripped off

The overcharges that actually happen to visitors — and the one habit that stops almost every one of them.

Almost every tourist overcharge in China follows one pattern: a friendly stranger starts the conversation, and the price only appears at the end. Ask the price first — before you sit, before you eat, before you get in.

The tea ceremony invitation. The most common one, and the most expensive. A well-dressed young person near a big shopping street says they are an art student and invites you to see a gallery or drink tea with their teacher. The bill arrives at 800–3,000 RMB. A local never pays to drink tea with a stranger. Decline warmly and keep walking.

Unlicensed taxis. At station exits and airport arrivals, men will ask "taxi? taxi?" before you reach the official rank. Their price is two to four times the meter. Use the official queue, or order on Didi — the route and fare stay recorded in the app.

The cheap day tour. A 99 RMB "Great Wall tour" includes two shopping stops where the guide earns commission on jade, silk or pearls. Book tours whose description says no shopping, and leave any tour that opens with a "factory".

Two menus. Some restaurants near major sights keep an English menu at higher prices. Ask for the Chinese menu too and compare, or order by photo in the app. Prices must legally be displayed — if they are not, that is your answer.

Weight and "free" gifts. At markets, watch the scale and agree the price per jin (500 g) before anything is bagged. A "free" photo at a scenic spot usually means a paid print; a "free" bracelet usually ends in a donation request.

Tickets. Popular sites (Forbidden City, panda bases, big museums) sell timed tickets through official WeChat mini-programs only. Anyone offering a paper ticket at the gate is either overcharging or reselling an ID-linked ticket you will not be able to use.

Insider tip: Type the number into your phone calculator and turn the screen toward them — "多少钱?" (duōshǎo qián, how much?). It removes the language gap and leaves a record of the agreed price. Rough anchors so nothing feels like a trap: a bowl of noodles 15–30 RMB, bottled water 2–3, metro ride 3–7, taxi flagfall 13–16, a pot of tea in a park teahouse 15–40, Forbidden City 40–60, Mutianyu Great Wall 45 plus shuttle.

Practice, not just sightseeing

Learn the 24-Form Tai Chi every Chinese park runs on

Walk into any park before 8am and you will see it: the 24-Form simplified Tai Chi, standardised in 1956 so ordinary people could learn a full routine in a few weeks. This is a back-view follow-along version by @气派太极-健身 on Bilibili — filmed from behind on purpose, so you do not have to mirror anything, you just follow. We added English names for all 24 forms and English cues for the breathing and hand movements. The original Chinese captions stay on screen, so you can match them line for line.

Why this one

It is the version grandmothers and office workers both do. Twenty-four short forms, eight minutes, no equipment, no gym, no fee. If you learn one thing in China that you can keep doing at home, learn this.

How to start

Watch once without moving. Then stand up and follow the video three times in a row: once to learn the arms, once to learn the steps, once to let the breath find its place. Two or three sessions a week beats one heroic hour.

The only rule

Breathe first, move second. “Inhale” cues open or lift; “exhale” cues settle, press or push. If you get out of breath, slow down — the video will wait for you.
